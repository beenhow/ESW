For years, I thought I truly knew my mother. But lately, while working on my second novel, I've begun to wonder how someone so close, whose voice I've known since before I had a name, could still remain, in so many ways, unknowable.
This realisation surfaced with the hardest part of the novel—the mother. She kept emerging as an image impossible to bring into focus. When I tried to press closer, I was running up against the bright, blinding presence of "mum." It was like trying to stare at the sun.
Our relationship has never been simple. It's not just love and warmth that defines us, but an ongoing push and pull where her desire to guide meets my resistance to being directed. Conversations can turn sharp without warning. And yet, even in those moments of quiet friction, I recognise the same fact: the impossibility of fully seeing each other beyond the roles we've both learned to assume.
My mother once told me she only ever wanted to be two things: a police officer and a mother, and became both, though she left the police force when she had her first baby. I press her on having sacrificed (牺牲) her career, especially as my own has taken priority over child rearing (养育). "I gave it up happily," she insists. A soft and important tension arises between what I define as worthy and what she feels is meaningful.
When I visit my friend's newborn son, I speak to her about her experience of becoming a mother. "There's a pathway between you that doesn't close after birth. It's symbiotic with pain. When my son feels pain, I feel it, too. That's why I must conceal (隐藏) mine." I talk to my mother about this instinct (本能). She admits, "Of course I've hidden my pain. It was painful when you left for university. Knowing that you would never come back as the same person."
Now, looking over the final pages of my novel, I understand it's precisely the mother's refusal to fully reveal herself to her daughters that gives her character its truth, mirroring the way I experience my own mother in life.
Some parts of our mothers are meant to remain just out of view. They hold part of themselves back so we can move forward.

Fish staying in large groups enjoy advantages that they lack on their own. For instance, when one fish notices a threat, it suddenly darts (猛冲) in a new direction. That information quickly spreads through the whole school, which can then escape together.
But Andrew Hein, a computational biologist from Cornell, was struck by how often a fish got things wrong. "It's safe," he said. "But all of a sudden, it will just flee for its life. Soon many fish are trying to escape together, from nothing."
The observation inspired Dr. Hein and his colleagues to investigate how information in the natural world can turn into misinformation. They developed mathematical models to estimate the accuracy of the beliefs of an organism (有机体) and the extent to which information from other organisms changes their beliefs.
Exploring these models, they came to the conclusion that misinformation is probably fundamental to all communication systems in the natural world.
Previously, some biologists looked at misinformation as a minor annoyance. If a fish darts away for no reason, it loses some time it could have spent eating. But that's a small cost, outweighed by the benefit of being able to escape predators.
Dr. Hein, however, believes that fish reacting to too many false alarms can risk their survival. "The cost isn't missing one lunch. It's missing all lunches."
Walter Quattrociocchi, an Italian data scientist, who was not involved in the study, agreed. "It shows that misinformation is not an odd thing or a moral failure, but a structural consequence of communication systems operating under noise, limited context and imperfect decoding (解码)."
The threat of misinformation has led to the development of defenses against it. In his research, Dr. Hein found that fish don't deal with misinformation by getting smart and they don't fact-check each signal they receive from other fish. When the animals swim in small groups, they are highly responsive to the movements of the fish around them. But in bigger groups, their brains become less sensitive. It takes the movement of many more fish to get them moving.
"We clearly need more ideas for how to cope with misinformation as a social species," Dr. Hein said. "Maybe it's possible to find some if we look at other species."

The English writer G. K. Chesterton identified the core problem with "disruptive (颠覆性的) innovation" through a thought experiment that has become known as "Chesterton's Fence."
Imagine there is a fence set up along a road for some purpose. But that purpose has been long forgotten. Now, the fence seems to be nothing but a barrier. Why not remove it to make life better?
To Chesterton, the fact we can't see the point of the fence is exactly why we should not tear it down. Some fences keep foxes out of henhouses, for instance. If you think you can do without the fence because you've never seen a fox, consider if the existence of the fence is precisely why you've never seen one.
If you think something has been done in an awkward way, and want to make changes, stop and ask why on earth someone would choose to do such a thing. The mere fact we don't immediately appreciate past decisions isn't a good enough reason to assume our ancestors were foolish or incompetent. If we refuse to see this, and thus move fast and break things, we may cause larger problems than the ones we claim to solve.
But herein lies a trap. If we're over-eager for innovation, we can end up tearing out things we don't yet realise we rely upon. But sticking to the "way things are done" can also take away the possibility of better days.
So how do we know when to disrupt and when to leave things be? This is a task of what the Greeks called practical wisdom, for which there is no checklist, only years of experience and the memory of good decisions—and the weight of bad ones—to apply to the novel situation in front of you.
That adds another complication: we, too, are building our own fences every time we do this. We're adding to the decisions that seem sensible (合理的) now but will be unclear to those coming after us. Perhaps one day people of the far future will look upon our works and despair (绝望)—at our silliness.
Disruption can be a force for good. It can also just leave a big mess. Wisdom is the ability to work out which messes are worth avoiding—because however impressively fast you move, some things can't be easily unbroken.

One afternoon, we set out for a run along the trails near my home. At first, everything felt effortless—the rhythm of my feet on the ground, the way Lindy kept looking back to make sure I was still with him. Suddenly, I felt a wave of dizziness. My legs failed. My blood sugar was crashing.

And then, Lindy did something extraordinary. He threw his head back and rooed. ROOOO! ROOOO! It was as if he were calling for help.
A woman running nearby slowed down. "Hey! Is he okay?" she asked, eyeing Lindy as he continued his call.
I tried to speak, but my lips barely moved.
"Blood sugar… low…"
Her face changed instantly.
"Oh—wait! I have something."
She reached into her pocket and pulled out a candy bar, pressing it into my hand. With shaking fingers, I tore it open, taking a bite. The sugar hit my bloodstream like fuel to an empty tank.
My vision cleared. My breath steadied (恢复平稳). Lindy finally stopped rooing, his job done. He pressed his warm body against mine, standing guard.
"I think your dog just saved your life," said the woman.
When I adopted Lindy, I thought I was rescuing him—from a life of cages and concrete, from the possibility of being discarded when he was no longer profitable. But in the end, he rescued me.
Thank you, my dear friend Lindy. I love you forever and a day.
