I walked into the memory care center holding my flute (长笛) tightly, nervous energy making my hands sweat. As I began setting up in the common room, an elderly man looked up from his chair. His eyes were kind but distant, the way eyes get when the foggy mind wanders.
"I used to…play the flute…at Carnegie Hall," he said slowly.
The words hung in the air. This man sitting before me, struggling to hold onto his own storyline, had once stood on one of the world's most celebrated stages. His name was Don.
That afternoon, I played for the residents. The notes danced along in the air, and I watched faces change. Some closed their eyes; others swayed (摇摆) gently. And I noticed something deep. Music reaches places that words cannot.
The visit planted a seed. What if this wasn't just a one-time visit? Why not create something bigger? Hence the organization, Our Ode To You, where student musicians, dancers, and artists were gathered. Our method, unlike those of other such organizations, was simple but intentional: create with residents rather than just perform at them.
Then came the day we brought extra instruments to the center. I brought an old and unused violin. Don's eyes landed on it. His weathered hands reached out. For a moment, nothing happened…until his fingers found their positions. Then, he began to play, perfectly and beautifully. His fingers remembered what his mind had forgotten. Tears streamed down his face as music reconnected him to his core identity as an artist, despite dementia (痴呆症). We all witnessed that the same person was still there, awaiting the right key to unlock his spirit.
The beauty of this work is its two-way benefit. Our volunteers arrive thinking they're helping, but they leave a reshaped version of themselves: they learn aging doesn't lower worth; they discover that wisdom isn't found only in textbooks but in the people's experiences; they understand that everyone deserves respect regardless of cognitive (认知的) ability.
Don still plays sometimes. And when he does, we're reminded why we do this work: everyone has the right to feel like themselves again, even if just for the length of one perfect tune.

Many dog owners will tell you that their dogs seem to anticipate dinnertime or regular walking time to within minutes. But time, measured in seconds, minutes, and hours, is a human invention. Do dogs actually have the idea of time in their memory like we do?
Researchers often break down long-term memory into implicit memory and declarative memory. Implicit memory is a pure muscle memory we use to perform tasks we have learned and repeated many times in the past, while declarative memory is where we store the personal experiences and factual information that make up the story of our lives.
Pavlov taught us that dogs and other animals have implicit memories, which are used for trial-and-error learning or conditioned responses. But there's still some uncertainty regarding whether they have declarative memory. Researchers are eager to figure out if animals have episodic memory (one form of declarative memory), or the ability to recall contextual information about past events, such as what was happening, or where and when it occurred. Prof. Stewart described such ability as a type of mental time travel.
The outline of tests on animals' episodic memory is fairly simple. Let's say you give a chimpanzee (大猩猩) two sealed (密封的) lightproof jars: one contains a frozen ball of juice that's going to melt (融化); the other has a non-melting grape. After five minutes, the chimpanzee is given the chance to open one jar. After an hour, it can open the other. Once the test has been repeated several times, most of them will break open the jar containing the disappearing treat first. They have demonstrated the three w's. What: a tasty treat. Where: inside this jar. When: within five minutes or it won't be around anymore.
While evidence suggests that dogs remember people and events, it's still an open question whether their minds can re-experience the past. That leads us again to the question: Can dogs actually tell time? Here is one educated guess made by researchers as to how dogs may do it if they can: dogs may use their advanced sense of smell to determine how long it has been since something happened. If you have a fairly regular schedule, it's possible your dog has figured out that at the point when your body smell has decreased by a specific amount, he needs to get ready to come charging up to greet you.

Why can't many people finish anything? That's the question I ask myself whenever I sit down to write a book that is overdue. It's perfectly fine for projects to be incomplete, as it takes time to do pretty much anything of value. And a bunch of half-done projects don't have to be stressful—in a certain state of mind, they can even be an inspiring testament (证据) to your ambition and imagination. Eventually, though, once a line gets crossed, the not-yet-finished becomes simply the unfinished.
There are many reasons for things ending up unfinished. In one common pattern, the skills needed to wrap something up turn out to be quite different from the ones needed to do the main part of the work: you've done a lot, but simply don't know how to do what's left. In another, a self-critical impulse (冲动) takes hold, and what seemed good at first is suddenly embarrassing. Your strength can be another issue: perhaps you just get tired or bored before pushing things through. And it's also possible to be a victim of your own success: you have created something so good that you have no idea how to follow it up and get stuck.
One obvious way to complete things is to get pushed from outside. It's true that my book is overdue—and yet, being overdue is actually a blessed state, thanks to the existing deadline. Because there stands an editor waiting for my final draft, the book is not-yet-finished, rather than unfinished.
We've all tried to set deadlines ourselves. It's easy enough to write one next to an item on your to-do list, or to enter one into whatever app you use to organize your life. Oddly, this is an almost philosophical act, given that it forces you to face the nature of reality. The difference between a real deadline and a self-set one is like the difference between eating breakfast and seeing it online. To create a real deadline, you must generate real accountability. This is by no means impossible: a monthly book-club meeting might be real enough to help you finish more books. But the more complex, ambitious your project is, the harder it can be to find a real reason to do it.
However, finishing things the hard way is often valuable precisely because it ends up in ways you can't anticipate. Wrapping up any particular project can require learning things that are context-bound, and acquiring skills for which you didn't sign up. There might be a sense in which finishing, in general, is a skill you can build. But what you're really developing is patience with the twists and turns (迂回曲折).

"Mama, this basketball game after school is super important. I need you there."
Like any proud mama, I promised I wouldn't miss it. When I got to work, I told my colleagues at the university I'd be leaving early to make it.
But as the day unfolded, I got caught in last-minute requests and quick questions, and before I knew it, I was running very late. By the time I was finally at the high school gym, the game was already in the fourth quarter.
After the buzzer (终场哨), my son came over to the stands and told me how glad he was that I made it. Then, with zero ill will, he asked me, "Have you ever been late for a meeting, a class, or even a flight?" I admitted to him—never, because those things are important.
He looked at me with sadness in his eyes and his voice, and asked, "But you can be late for me?"
That moment struck an emotional blow to me. He was right. The way I was showing up didn't align with the values I claimed to hold most dear. If family was what I valued most, my actions suggested otherwise.
My son gave me a gift that day: he reminded me that change is possible and trusted I'd do better. From then on, I made it non-negotiable to keep my word when it came to showing up for my family and the things of highest importance.
The reality is that so many of us live such full lives that it can feel difficult to make room for what we deeply value.
Often, it takes a moment of deep reflection for people to realize how far they've drifted (偏离) from what they value most. However, it's never too late to realign with our values. When we achieve it, our days feel richer. And the beautiful thing is, we give others permission to live within their values, too.
